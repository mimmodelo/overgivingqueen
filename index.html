Overcoming overgiving in relationships

When caring starts costing you

Overgiving can look like love — until you disappear.

You might be generous, thoughtful, and deeply loyal. But if you routinely ignore your own limits to keep the peace or feel valued, your care may be coming at your expense.

01

You say yes when you mean no

You agree to things to avoid disappointment, tension, or the fear that someone will pull away.

02

You carry the emotional load

You monitor everyone's feelings, initiate every repair, and feel responsible for keeping the relationship okay.

03

Your needs come last

You minimize what you want, then feel unseen, resentful, or exhausted when it goes unnoticed.

"You don't have to earn love by being endlessly available."

There is a difference between giving from the heart and giving up yourself.

A small shift can begin here

From automatic giving to intentional care.

This isn't about becoming less caring. It's about including yourself in the care you offer.

1

Notice the moment

Pause before the automatic yes. What are you feeling, and what are you afraid might happen if you say no?

2

Name what you need

Give your needs the same attention you give other people's. Rest, time, reassurance, and respect count.

3

Practice one honest boundary

Start small. A clear, kind limit can help you discover which relationships make room for the real you.

Good to know

Questions, answered.

Is this for me if I'm not sure I overgive?

Yes. The book and course help you reflect on your own patterns without labeling or judging yourself. Take what is useful and move at your own pace.

Does setting boundaries mean becoming selfish?

No. Boundaries help clarify what you can offer willingly and sustainably. You can be compassionate while also being honest about your limits.

Will this fix my relationship?

No resource can promise a particular relationship outcome. These materials focus on your awareness, choices, and communication. Relationships also depend on the other person's behavior.

Is this therapy?

No. These are educational resources, not mental health treatment or a substitute for professional support.
